# President's Address
## 36th Biennial General Conference of MCTA Shillong

Honourable dignitaries, distinguished guests, respected colleagues, dear teachers, and friends,

It is both a privilege and a profound responsibility to address this 36th Biennial Conference of the Meghalaya College Teachers' Association.

As teachers, we are accustomed to speaking about education in terms of classrooms, examinations, syllabi, degrees and careers. However, I believe that, at this moment in our country's and state's history, we must view education from a broader perspective.

Education is not merely a department of government. Education is not merely an expenditure in a budget. Education is an investment in the economic, social and democratic future of a nation.

The Economic Survey of India for 2025–26 itself describes education as “the core pillar of human capital” and as central to India's growth trajectory towards Viksit Bharat 2047.

That is a significant observation.

Because when a government spends on a road, it creates physical infrastructure. When it invests in a power project, it creates productive infrastructure. However, when a nation invests in education, it develops the human infrastructure necessary to make all other types of infrastructure productive.

The young population which our country possesses provides us an extraordinary economic opportunity—the opportunity that we commonly describe as the demographic dividend.

However, a young population is not, by itself, a demographic dividend.

It becomes a dividend only when young people are educated, healthy, skilled, productive and able to secure meaningful employment.

Therefore, when we discuss expenditure on education, we are not discussing a narrow issue concerning teachers and educational institutions alone.

We are discussing India's future productivity.

We are discussing our capacity to innovate.

We are discussing whether our young people will become job seekers or job creators.

We are discussing whether India can transform its demographic advantage into sustained economic growth.

The numbers therefore deserve our attention.

The Government of India records that public expenditure on education by the Centre and states has remained below the 6 per cent of GDP level envisaged in the NEP 2020. The latest official consolidated data available for 2022–23 put expenditure by education and other departments at approximately 4.06 per cent of GDP.

In Meghalaya, as per the claims of the government's budget documents, the allocation to the Education Department for 2025–26 was ₹3,654 crore, including ₹2,770 crore from state resources. Of the total allocation, ₹1,403 crore was earmarked for grant-in-aid salaries and ₹170 crore for capital works.

These are substantial figures. But how much is the government of Meghalaya spending?

Is the government investing enough, and is it investing in the right places? Is that capital used to prepare the young people of Meghalaya for the economy of tomorrow?

Meghalaya has an important opportunity before it.

The All India Survey on Higher Education records Meghalaya's Gross Enrolment Ratio in higher education at 26.9 per cent in 2022–23, compared with 23.1 per cent in 2018–19.

That increase is encouraging. But it also tells us that a substantial proportion of our young people still do not enter higher education.

For those who enter college, another question arises: What kind of education are we providing, and what awaits them when they graduate?

This brings me to the implementation of the National Education Policy, 2020, and the FYUP in Meghalaya.

Our Association has consistently supported the objective of improving the quality, flexibility and relevance of higher education. We should not be against reform merely because it is a reform.

Our own colleges—particularly colleges serving rural and economically disadvantaged communities—face practical constraints.

The document placed before this conference rightly draws attention to inadequate infrastructure, shortage of qualified and adequate manpower for additional courses, and concerns about a fragmented curriculum.

We therefore have to ask:

Can we expect a world-class curriculum without world-class infrastructure?

Can we introduce additional courses without adequate teachers?

Can we promise multidisciplinary education without the academic manpower to teach multiple disciplines?

Can we demand research and innovation from institutions without providing the laboratories, libraries, research support and time necessary to undertake meaningful research?

And can we judge institutions solely by outcomes when the starting conditions of those institutions are fundamentally unequal?

These are not arguments against NEP. They are arguments for implementing NEP responsibly.

Our students should not become the experimental generation of educational reform.

They deserve a system in which policy, infrastructure, faculty and academic resources move together.

And this brings me to the most important constituency in this entire debate—the student.

Today, young individuals entering a college in Meghalaya confront a future filled with uncertainty.

The latest Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) provides a sobering indicator. For 2025, the unemployment rate among Meghalaya's 15–29-year-olds was 12.2% under the usual-status measure—8.3 % among young men and 18.4 % among young women.

What are we preparing our students for?

If we are preparing our students only for examinations and degrees, then we are not preparing them adequately.

Our educational institutions  must become places where students acquire not only knowledge, but also skills, critical thinking, communication ability, digital competence, entrepreneurial capacity, research capability and the confidence to create opportunities where opportunities do not exist.

This is where the teacher becomes indispensable. Budgets can create infrastructure.

But it is the teacher who converts educational investment into human capability.

And therefore, colleagues, the welfare of teachers is not a side issue.

Teacher welfare is education policy.

A teacher who is insecure about employment, uncertain about career progression, or worried about the future of his or her income stream cannot be expected to devote the best intellectual energy to teaching, research and mentoring.

This brings me to the Deficit Grant-in-Aid system, which has played a distinctive role in the development of education in Meghalaya.

Our conference document reminds us of the historic importance of this system and its contribution to the growth of colleges across the state.

Whatever our differences regarding policy, one principle should command broad agreement:

The future of public-interest higher education in Meghalaya cannot be determined solely by market considerations.

Education, like health, has profound social benefits that extend far beyond the individual who receives it.

An educated teacher educates hundreds of students.

An educated student contributes to a family and community.

A college creates not merely graduates but citizens, professionals, researchers, entrepreneurs and leaders.

The return on education therefore extends far beyond the balance sheet of an individual institution.

This is why any proposed restructuring of grants and funding mechanisms must be approached with transparency, consultation and safeguards.

The document before this conference refers to the Meghalaya Education Grant proposal and raises concerns about future grant structures and performance-linked indicators such as enrolment, fee structures, infrastructure and institutional performance.

Performance and accountability are important.

But performance cannot be separated from circumstances.

A college serving a remote rural population cannot necessarily be assessed by exactly the same indicators as a well-resourced urban institution.

Enrolment cannot be treated simply as a market statistic.

Fees cannot become a substitute for public investment.

And institutional autonomy must be accompanied by institutional capacity.

Therefore, as representatives of the teaching community, we call for a funding framework that provides stability, transparency, equity and accountability.

We seek assurance that no existing institution will be placed in jeopardy because of a sudden change in the grant mechanism.

We seek meaningful consultation with teachers and institutions before major structural changes are introduced.

We seek adequate funding for infrastructure and faculty development.

And above all, we seek a higher education policy that puts the student and the public interest at its centre.

Colleagues,

The challenges before Meghalaya's education system are formidable.

Our task as teachers is not simply to criticise government.

Our task is to hold the government to the promise of education—and to help build the education system that Meghalaya deserves.

And we need a serious state-level strategy for employment creation so that our educated young people can see a future within Meghalaya, rather than being compelled to leave in search of one.

Because ultimately, the test of our education system will not be the number of policies we announce.

It will be whether the young person from a village in Meghalaya can enter a college, receive a quality education, develop confidence and skills, and graduate believing:

**“There is a place for me in the future of this state.”**

That, colleagues, must be our collective mission.

Let us therefore defend education not as a privilege for the few, but as a public good and a public responsibility.

Let us defend the dignity of the teaching profession.

Let us demand that educational reforms be properly funded and responsibly implemented.

Let us insist that accountability should strengthen institutions rather than weaken them.

And let us remember that behind every budget figure, every policy document and every institutional statistic stands a young person whose future depends upon the choices

The demographic dividend of India will be created in our classrooms and in many hallowed halls of colleges. And, above all, it will be created through the commitment of teachers who continue to believe that education can transform an individual, a community and a nation.

Let us therefore invest in education.
Let us invest in our teachers.
Let us invest in our students.
And, through them, let us invest in the future of Meghalaya.

Thank you. Long Live MCTA. Long Live Teachers’ unity.
